## AI-writing patterns

The purpose of this section is editorial: improve generic or synthetic prose. Do not use it to defeat authorship checks, conceal academic misconduct, or fabricate evidence that a person wrote something they did not.

### Diagnose clusters, not tokens

No single word, punctuation mark, or sentence proves AI authorship. Look for several patterns reinforcing one another:

- every paragraph has the same length and shape;
- sentences settle into a uniform medium rhythm;
- each list contains exactly three items whether the material calls for two or five;
- claims are grander than the evidence;
- transitions announce importance without adding information;
- abstract nouns replace people, objects, and actions;
- conclusions repeat the introduction in brighter language;
- the prose offers generic balance without committing to a useful judgment;
- headings are generic containers such as “Challenges and Opportunities” or “The Road Ahead”;
- the response ends with a cheerful offer to do more.

### Common formulas

Inspect phrases such as:

- “plays a crucial/pivotal role”
- “stands as a testament to”
- “serves as a reminder”
- “underscores the importance of”
- “marks a significant milestone”
- “a rich tapestry of”
- “in today's rapidly evolving landscape”
- “not merely X, but Y”
- “it is not about X; it is about Y”
- “by doing X, organizations can…”
- “ultimately, the key lies in…”
- “the future looks promising”

The phrase may be legitimate. Ask whether it says more than the surrounding facts. If not, replace the whole thought, not only the flagged words.

### A five-step humanizing pass

1. **Recover the intended claim.** State what the paragraph actually means in plain language.
2. **Add owned material.** Use a real observation, decision, example, limitation, or consequence.
3. **Change the rhythm.** Let short and long sentences follow the thought rather than a template.
4. **Remove performance.** Cut inflated significance, canned transitions, false balance, and decorative conclusions.
5. **Read aloud.** Rewrite any line a thoughtful speaker would not naturally say.

Preserve productive irregularity: a precise aside, a mixed feeling, a local expression, an unresolved tension, or a detail too inconvenient to be marketing copy.

## Clarity and living English

### Think before simplifying

Muddy prose often reflects a muddy decision. If a sentence resists revision, ask:

- What happened?
- Who did it?
- Who is affected?
- What should the reader do or understand?
- What evidence supports the claim?

Do not hunt for synonyms until those answers are clear.

### Restore people and actions

English bureaucratese often hides action inside nouns:

| Foggy | Clear |
| --- | --- |
| We conducted an assessment of the proposal. | We assessed the proposal. |
| Implementation of the policy will occur in June. | We will introduce the policy in June. |
| A decision was made to suspend service. | The operations team suspended service. |
| The provision of support is our responsibility. | We support customers. |
| There was a failure to complete the review. | The committee did not finish the review. |

Passive voice is useful when the actor is unknown, irrelevant, or deliberately backgrounded. It is harmful when it conceals responsibility or makes a simple event hard to picture.

### Replace vague praise with material

“World-class,” “seamless,” “robust,” “unique,” “innovative,” and “customer-centric” ask the reader to accept the author's verdict. Replace them with one of four forms:

1. **Fact:** response time, term, price, capacity, condition, result.
2. **Scenario:** what the reader can now do in a real situation.
3. **Demonstration:** screenshot, sample, comparison, trial, photograph.
4. **Story:** a real person, problem, action, and outcome.

Test generic copy by hiding the company name. If a competitor could claim every sentence unchanged, the copy lacks evidence.

### Remove clutter with judgment

Common clutter includes “actually,” “basically,” “generally,” “in terms of,” “with respect to,” “the fact that,” “it should be noted that,” and “for the purpose of.” Remove or rewrite them when they add no distinction.

Do not treat a watchlist as a ban. “Actually” can mark a genuine correction. “In general” can distinguish a broad rule from an exception. Keep a word when it performs a real job.

### Build clean sentences and paragraphs

- Give a sentence one main action.
- Keep the main subject and verb near each other.
- Present events in the order they occurred unless a deliberate reveal requires otherwise.
- Break deeply nested qualifications into separate sentences.
- Give each paragraph one topic and a first sentence that helps a scanning reader.
- Use lists only for genuinely parallel items.
- Order list entries by the reader's search task, not by the author's discovery process.

## Structure and genre

### Choose a structure that matches the material

- **News:** event → essential details → background.
- **Story:** person → problem → actions and reversals over time → consequence.
- **Instruction:** context and outcome → chronological steps → checks and recovery.
- **Collection or ranking:** consistent modules with useful, parallel headings.
- **Situation overview:** several distinct views of one subject.
- **Argument:** claim → evidence → counterpoint → conclusion.

For a long piece, make a compact contract with the reader: what this is, what it covers, and what they will be able to do or understand afterward.

### Email and messages

Put the decision, request, or problem in the first paragraph. Include the reason only to the extent that it helps the recipient decide or act. Make the next step explicit: who does what, by when, and what happens if they cannot.

For bad news:

1. state the news plainly;
2. own the relevant responsibility;
3. explain briefly without turning the explanation into a defense;
4. give consequences, options, and the next update.

### Company and product pages

Open with the product or company category, intended customer, and practical benefit. Use “therefore” as a test: “We do X; therefore the customer can Y.” If the second half is empty, the first half is probably self-description rather than value.

A useful landing-page sequence is:

1. what the product is and who it is for;
2. three to five evidence-backed benefits;
3. details, constraints, comparisons, and answers to objections;
4. price or terms and a clear action.

Do not hide the price, material conditions, or a known limitation behind a contact form or vague promise.

### Reports and decision documents

Do not merely shorten a complex report. Reorganize it:

1. group material under descriptive headings;
2. put the conclusion first in each section;
3. place the few decision-relevant points at the top;
4. state what action or decision is needed.

Use tables or diagrams when they make comparisons or relationships easier to inspect. Do not scatter bold text through paragraphs as a substitute for structure.

### Instructions and documentation

Tell the reader the outcome, prerequisites, and risks before the steps. Use one action per step. Show the expected result after a risky or ambiguous step. Include recovery for the failures most likely to occur. Do not bury warnings after the action they govern.

### Resumes and applications

Answer the employer's requirements with evidence: “I can do X; here is where I did it and what changed.” State a material gap honestly. Put personal history and interests after the proof, not before it. Avoid fan letters, forced creativity, and autobiography from childhood onward.

### Cold outreach

Keep it to two to five short paragraphs:

1. a specific sign that the message is genuinely for this person;
2. a problem or opportunity relevant to them;
3. a modest acknowledgment that your reading may be wrong;
4. useful work done in advance, when appropriate;
5. a low-effort next step.

Do not fake familiarity, urgency, or personalization.

## Craft techniques

### Openings

Begin with the answer, event, image, conflict, or useful surprise. Avoid universal truths, throat-clearing, dictionary definitions, and “Since the dawn of time.” A good opening makes a promise the rest of the piece fulfills.

When revising, try removing the first paragraph. Many drafts begin one paragraph before the real beginning.

### Endings

Stop after the strongest necessary line. Useful endings may:

- complete an image introduced at the start;
- reveal the consequence of the argument;
- give one specific next action;
- introduce a final fact that changes how the piece is understood.

Avoid “In conclusion,” motivational fog, and a summary that adds nothing.

### Detail and abstraction

Move down the ladder of abstraction until the reader can see an object, action, choice, or scene. Then move back up to explain why it matters. A detail earns its place when it advances the story, establishes evidence, reveals character, or changes interpretation. A detail that does none of these is clutter.

### Rhythm and emphasis

Sentence length should follow thought and emotion. Put new or forceful information near the end of a sentence. Use repetition only when it creates deliberate pressure or pattern. Read aloud for breath, stress, and accidental monotony.

### Scenes and dialogue

A scene needs a person who wants something, an obstacle, a place, and change. Use dialogue for choices and tension, not for information both speakers already know. Do not polish quoted speech into an elegance the speaker did not possess.

### Editing another person's work

Read the whole piece before changing it. Identify the promise and the author's voice. Edit the structure before the wording. Preserve choices you can understand and defend, even if they are not choices you would make in your own prose. Explain high-impact changes; do not overwhelm the author with every possible preference.

## Invention and writer's block

Writer's block is often a conflict among fear, perfectionism, missing material, and a task that is too large to enter.

### Lower the cost of starting

- Allow an intentionally bad first draft.
- Set a short time box and stop when it ends.
- Begin with the scene, fact, or sentence that has energy, even if it belongs in the middle.
- Say the piece aloud before writing it.
- Separate research, drafting, and editing sessions.

Do not shame someone for a weak draft. Harsh evaluation during generation teaches the writer to stop generating.

### Find a grain of sand

Start from one thing that catches: an image, contradiction, overheard phrase, fact, object, or question. Ask what happened before it, what could happen after it, and who would care most.

### Generators for ideas

- **Distant pair:** combine two unrelated nouns and invent the connection.
- **What if:** change one rule of the ordinary world and follow the consequences.
- **Reversal:** tell a familiar story from the opponent's position or reverse its moral expectation.
- **Constraint:** limit the setting, time, cast, or available action.
- **Escalation:** repeat a situation with one meaningful change each time.
- **Hidden cost:** give the desired outcome an unexpected but coherent price.

Use generators to create material, then select. Do not force every generated idea into the final piece.

## Usage and fact-checking

### Usage

Prefer established, current English that fits the audience and region. Check a reputable dictionary or style guide when spelling, inflection, idiom, capitalization, or usage could affect credibility. Do not “correct” dialect, identity language, or an author's deliberate voice merely because it differs from one prestige standard.

Keep terminology when it is exact and useful to the intended reader. Explain it when the reader needs the concept but may not know the term. Plain language is not a ban on technical language.

### Facts and quotations

- Verify names, dates, numbers, titles, and links.
- Distinguish observed fact, reported claim, inference, and opinion.
- Preserve uncertainty present in the evidence.
- Trace statistics to their source and denominator.
- Do not invent or silently repair quotations.
- If a claim cannot be verified, remove it, qualify it, or tell the user what evidence is missing.

## Emotion and contact

Strong emotion reaches the body before reasoning. When someone is angry, frightened, or flooded, arguments often fail because the person cannot yet process them.

### A useful sequence

1. Let them finish.
2. Name the apparent emotion without claiming certainty: “It sounds like that left you feeling cornered.”
3. Reflect the event or concern in their terms.
4. Ask one question that helps them continue or correct your understanding.
5. Discuss options only after they feel accurately heard.

Acknowledging emotion does not concede blame or accept an inaccurate account. It establishes contact so the substance can be discussed.

### When someone shuts down

Reduce pressure. Replace demands for an immediate explanation with an observation and a choice: “You've gone quiet. We can pause, or you can tell me what part feels hardest to answer.” Allow silence. Do not fill it with a lecture.

### Tone

Warmth comes from attention, not exclamation marks. Use the person's name sparingly. Admit uncertainty plainly. Avoid canned praise such as “Great question” unless there is a specific reason to praise the question.

## Difficult conversations

A hard conversation usually contains three conversations at once:

1. **What happened:** each person has a different story about facts, intent, and blame.
2. **Feelings:** emotions influence the exchange whether named or not.
3. **Identity:** the conflict threatens how each person sees themselves.

Prepare all three. Separate intent from impact: you may not know what they meant, but you can describe what happened and how it affected you.

### A practical opening

Use a neutral description, your interpretation as an interpretation, and an invitation:

> We agreed on Friday, and the file arrived Monday. I read that as a change in priority, but I may be missing something. What happened on your side?

Avoid opening with a verdict about character or motive.

### Feedback

Name the observable behavior, its effect, and the desired next action. Keep praise specific and free of a trailing “but.” If criticism is necessary, address the work privately and give the person a usable path forward.

### Bad news

Do not hide the news in a compliment sandwich. State it, acknowledge the effect, own your part, and explain the next practical steps. Do not demand that the recipient feel better immediately.

## Negotiation and boundaries

### Interests before positions

A position is what someone says they want; an interest is why it matters. Ask what problem the position solves, what constraint produced it, and what would make another option acceptable.

Separate the people from the problem. Generate options before choosing among them. Use external criteria—market rates, policy, precedent, measurable scope—when possible.

### Know the alternative

Before negotiating, identify the best realistic alternative if no agreement is reached. This prevents desperation from setting the terms. Do not bluff about an alternative you do not have.

### Saying no

A clear refusal can include:

1. the decision;
2. a short reason, if useful;
3. an alternative you genuinely offer;
4. a boundary against repeated pressure.

Do not apologize repeatedly or turn “no” into a long defense. A refusal is not an invitation to keep persuading unless the person says it is.

### Pressure tactics

Slow the exchange when someone creates artificial urgency, false scarcity, guilt, or a choice between two options they selected. Ask what changes if you decide later, request the terms in writing, and bring the conversation back to interests and criteria.

## Influence, reinforcement, and leadership

### Ethical influence

Several mechanisms affect decisions automatically: reciprocity, consistency, social proof, liking, authority, scarcity, and shared identity. Use them only with true signals:

- give real value before asking;
- cite real behavior by a relevant group;
- show genuine expertise and its limits;
- state real capacity or deadlines;
- name real common ground.

Fabricated reviews, countdowns, popularity, credentials, or belonging are deception.

### Reinforcement

To strengthen useful behavior, notice the specific action close to when it happens. For a complex skill, reward progress toward the result rather than waiting for perfection. Do not attach criticism to praise with “but”; the criticism erases the reward.

When behavior must change, consider whether the environment rewards it. Remove accidental rewards, make the preferred behavior easier, and reinforce the next correct step. Punishment often teaches concealment rather than improvement.

### Leadership

Leadership through language means making reality easier to see and action easier to take. State the purpose, constraints, decision rights, and next step. Give credit freely and responsibility clearly. Confidence does not require pretending certainty; people trust leaders who distinguish what they know, what they believe, and what they are still testing.

## Final checks

Before delivering prose, ask:

- Does the first paragraph contain the point?
- Does every section serve the reader's purpose?
- Are actors and actions visible?
- Are claims supported by facts, examples, or honest qualification?
- Did the edit preserve voice and useful detail?
- What still sounds generated?
- Would the people affected feel accurately and fairly represented?
- Is the requested next action clear?
